Why do profitable-looking businesses run out of cash?

A plain-language way to separate sales, profit and cash, then identify where money is tied up.

Prepared by NeuroNest. Independent editorial review is still required.

Use this draft carefully.

No statistics or client results are claimed. Examples are invented and labelled. Adapt every routine to the business and applicable rules.

Sales, profit and cash answer different questions

Sales show what the business charged customers. Profit compares income with the costs assigned to earning it. Cash shows what money is actually available now. A business can record a profitable sale while still waiting for the customer to pay. It can also spend cash on stock that has not yet been sold.

Keep these three views separate. A busy till does not automatically mean the bank balance can cover rent, payroll or the next supplier order. The useful question is not only “Did we sell?” It is “When did the cash arrive, and what must leave before the next receipt?”

Draw a simple cash map

List the ways cash enters: cash sales, mobile money, bank transfers, customer debt payments and owner funding. Then list the ways it leaves: supplier payments, wages, rent, transport, tax, loan repayments, withdrawals and small operating purchases. Give each item a date, not only a monthly total.

This map reveals timing. A supplier may require payment on Monday while a large customer pays on Friday. The week can be profitable on paper and still short of cash for four days. Timing decisions belong in the operating plan, not at the end of the month.

Find money tied up in stock and credit

Unsold stock uses cash. Slow-moving products may look like value on a stock report but cannot pay a bill until they are sold and collected. Customer credit has the same effect. A sale entered today does not create usable cash if payment is due later or becomes overdue.

Review stock by age and movement. Review customer balances by due date. Do not combine every unpaid account into one number. Separate current amounts, overdue amounts and balances under dispute. That makes the next action clear: reorder less, follow up, correct an invoice or agree a payment plan.

Use a four-week cash view

Start with cash available today. For each of the next four weeks, list expected receipts that have a reasonable basis and payments that are due. Do not count a possible sale as confirmed cash. Mark uncertain receipts separately. Update the view when a customer pays or a supplier date changes.

Example: a shop starts with KES 80,000. It expects KES 60,000 of confirmed collections, but rent and supplier payments total KES 120,000 before those collections arrive. The example shows a timing gap of KES 40,000 before other expenses. It does not describe a NeuroNest customer or result. The owner can now negotiate dates, delay a non-essential purchase or accelerate a confirmed collection before the gap becomes a crisis.

Control withdrawals and hidden small costs

Owner withdrawals need a category and date. Without that record, the business may appear to lose cash even when the money was taken for personal use. Small purchases also add up: delivery fees, airtime, packaging, repairs and staff reimbursements should not live only in messages or memory.

Set a simple rule: every cash movement gets a reason, amount, date and responsible person. Reconcile cash, mobile money and bank balances separately. Investigate differences while the day is recent. Do not force the record to match by adding a vague adjustment.

A weekly review that leads to action

Once a week, review available cash, confirmed receipts, payments due, overdue customer balances, slow stock and owner withdrawals. Choose the two largest risks for the next fourteen days. Give each one an action and date. A report without an owner or next step will not improve the cash position.

The aim is earlier visibility. When sales, stock, credit and payments are recorded in connected but understandable views, the owner can see a shortage before the due date. The tool can be a spreadsheet or a tested system. The discipline is the same: dates, clear categories, realistic receipts and regular reconciliation.

Keep the record useful

Write down who owns the routine, when it happens and where the record lives. Use names for roles rather than leaving a task with “everyone”. Agree how a mistake is corrected so the original entry remains traceable. If the team needs a long explanation every day, simplify the form or report.

Review the routine after two weeks. Ask which fields are skipped, which numbers cannot be checked and which alerts produce no action. Remove information that has no decision attached, but keep evidence needed for reconciliation and accountability. Train the person who covers the role when the usual owner is absent.

Keep backups or exports appropriate to the tool, then test that a saved copy can actually be read. Limit access to the people who need it. The aim is a small, repeatable control the business can continue without constant supervision. A system can prompt the steps and preserve history, but the business still decides the rules, reviews exceptions and acts on what the record shows.
