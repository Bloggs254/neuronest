Why recorded stock and physical stock never match, and how to fix it

A method for tracing stock differences through units, timing, receipts, sales and adjustments.

Prepared by NeuroNest. Independent editorial review is still required.

Use this draft carefully.

No statistics or client results are claimed. Examples are invented and labelled. Adapt every routine to the business and applicable rules.

Treat the mismatch as a process signal

When physical stock and recorded stock disagree, changing the system number to the count may hide the cause. First preserve both numbers, the count time and the person who counted. The difference is evidence that one part of the movement process is missing, late or wrong.

Use a variance record with product, unit, expected quantity, counted quantity, difference and explanation status. “Adjusted” is not an explanation. Keep the original number available after a correction so the business can see repeated causes.

Check the unit of measure

The same product may be bought by carton, stored by pack and sold by piece. If the conversion is not fixed, each movement can introduce a difference. Record the base unit and every allowed conversion. Labels and product codes should distinguish sizes that look similar.

Example: one carton contains 24 bottles. A delivery of three cartons should add 72 bottles if the base unit is a bottle. Entering “3” creates a difference of 69 before any sale occurs. The example is invented to show the method. The correction is to record the conversion and make the entry screen clear.

Align the count with transaction timing

A count at 6 p.m. cannot be compared fairly with a report that includes sales until 8 p.m. Freeze movement during the count or record the exact cut-off. Include pending receipts, returns and transfers only when the physical movement has happened and the agreed record stage is complete.

Late entry creates a moving target. Set a deadline for receiving notes, damage records and transfers. If an item moves after the cut-off, place it in the next period rather than silently editing the earlier count.

Trace each movement type

Review supplier receipts against the delivery note and physical quantity. Review sales against the correct product code. Check returns, samples, internal use, damage, expiry, transfers and write-offs. A business that records purchases and sales but ignores the other movements will keep producing unexplained gaps.

Assign a simple reason code and require a short note for adjustments. Restrict who can backdate or delete a completed movement. If a correction is required, add a reversing entry or another visible correction instead of erasing history.

Use cycle counts instead of waiting

A full count can disrupt the business and may happen too rarely to trace a cause. Cycle counting checks a manageable group on a schedule. Count fast-moving or valuable products more often and stable products less often. Keep the schedule realistic enough to finish.

When a product repeatedly differs, increase its count frequency and inspect its workflow. When it remains stable, the business can spend attention elsewhere. The purpose is not to produce a perfect percentage; it is to find the point where the record stops matching reality.

Close the loop after correction

For each confirmed cause, choose one control: clearer labels, delivery double-checks, restricted adjustments, a damage station, a return form or better staff training. Give the action an owner and review date. If the same variance returns, the control was not followed or did not address the real cause.

Begin with twenty products and one week of movements. Count, investigate and correct the process before expanding. A reliable stock record is built through consistent units, aligned cut-offs, complete movement types and visible corrections. Software helps enforce those decisions, but it cannot decide them for the business.

Keep the record useful

Write down who owns the routine, when it happens and where the record lives. Use names for roles rather than leaving a task with “everyone”. Agree how a mistake is corrected so the original entry remains traceable. If the team needs a long explanation every day, simplify the form or report.

Review the routine after two weeks. Ask which fields are skipped, which numbers cannot be checked and which alerts produce no action. Remove information that has no decision attached, but keep evidence needed for reconciliation and accountability. Train the person who covers the role when the usual owner is absent.

Keep backups or exports appropriate to the tool, then test that a saved copy can actually be read. Limit access to the people who need it. The aim is a small, repeatable control the business can continue without constant supervision. A system can prompt the steps and preserve history, but the business still decides the rules, reviews exceptions and acts on what the record shows.
