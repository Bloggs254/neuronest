How do you know if stock is going missing?

A practical stock-counting routine for spotting unexplained gaps without accusing people or trusting one number.

Prepared by NeuroNest. Independent editorial review is still required.

Use this draft carefully.

No statistics or client results are claimed. Examples are invented and labelled. Adapt every routine to the business and applicable rules.

Start with a question you can check

A stock gap is the difference between what your record says should be available and what you can actually count. The gap does not tell you why it happened. A missed delivery, a sale entered twice, damaged goods, the wrong unit of measure or deliberate removal can all produce a similar number. Begin with the record, then investigate the cause. Do not begin by accusing a member of staff.

Choose a small group of products that matter enough to check often. High-value items, fast sellers and products that are easy to carry are useful starting points. Write down the exact item, pack size and counting unit. “Cooking oil” is too broad if the shop sells several bottle sizes. A precise list makes tomorrow’s comparison possible.

Create one daily movement record

For each selected product, record opening quantity, stock received, stock sold or issued, damaged or returned quantity and closing physical quantity. Use the same cut-off time every day. If the business closes at 8 p.m., count after the last sale is recorded and before anyone starts the next day’s work.

The expected closing quantity is opening stock plus receipts, minus sales and other recorded issues. Compare it with the physical count. Keep the calculation visible so another person can follow it. A spreadsheet, ruled book or a tested inventory tool can all work. The important part is one agreed sequence and one responsible person.

Count without disturbing the evidence

Pause movement of the selected products while counting. Count sealed cartons and loose units separately, then convert them into the agreed unit. If one carton contains twelve bottles, record whether the system expects cartons or bottles. Mixing those units is a common way to create a gap that is only a recording error.

Use a second count when the first result differs from the record. The second person should count without being told the expected number. If both counts match each other, review the transactions. If they differ, the counting method or product location needs attention.

Investigate in a fixed order

Check receipts first: was the supplier quantity confirmed, and was the correct product recorded? Then check sales, returns, damaged stock, transfers and staff-use items. Look for transactions entered on the wrong date or against a similar product. Record the explanation beside the gap instead of silently changing the closing number.

Example: the record expects 24 packets at close, but the physical count is 21. A review finds one damaged packet recorded in a notebook but not in the stock record, and two packets sold under a similar product code. Label this an example, not a business result. The useful outcome is the corrected process: damage and product-code errors now have a clear recording step.

Use patterns, not one dramatic count

A single difference may be a mistake. Repeated differences in the same product, shift, supplier delivery or transaction type deserve attention. Keep a short weekly summary showing the product, size of gap, explanation and action taken. Do not publish staff names in a shared report unless there is a legitimate reason and an agreed process.

If a gap cannot be explained, protect the records and escalate it privately. Limit who can edit completed transactions, require approval for adjustments, and keep a dated history. Cameras or biometric controls are separate decisions with privacy and employment implications; they are not a substitute for a clean stock record.

A routine to start this week

Pick ten products. Confirm their units and storage locations. Record opening balances on Monday, then count the same items at close for five working days. Review every difference the next morning while receipts and sales are still easy to trace. On Friday, choose one process change based on the repeated cause: clearer product codes, a damage log, delivery checks or restricted adjustments.

The goal is not a perfect-looking report. It is a record that shows where a difference came from and what will prevent it happening again. Start small enough to keep the routine. Expand only after the first group is counted consistently.

Keep the record useful

Write down who owns the routine, when it happens and where the record lives. Use names for roles rather than leaving a task with “everyone”. Agree how a mistake is corrected so the original entry remains traceable. If the team needs a long explanation every day, simplify the form or report.

Review the routine after two weeks. Ask which fields are skipped, which numbers cannot be checked and which alerts produce no action. Remove information that has no decision attached, but keep evidence needed for reconciliation and accountability. Train the person who covers the role when the usual owner is absent.

Keep backups or exports appropriate to the tool, then test that a saved copy can actually be read. Limit access to the people who need it. The aim is a small, repeatable control the business can continue without constant supervision. A system can prompt the steps and preserve history, but the business still decides the rules, reviews exceptions and acts on what the record shows.
